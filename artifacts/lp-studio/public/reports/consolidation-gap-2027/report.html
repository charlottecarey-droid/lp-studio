Executive summary

Consolidation has become an execution challenge.

Among the emerging dental organizations we surveyed, interest in reducing lab vendors is nearly universal. Active rollout is rare. The leadership question for 2027 is how to turn that intent into a repeatable operating capability.

01

Ambition is widespread. Implementation is not.

53% call fewer lab vendors an active priority and 43% are considering it. Of 110 respondents answering the stage question, 77% are evaluating options, 18% are planning or piloting and 1% are rolling out a plan.

02

Central purchasing has not standardized practice behavior.

Every respondent describes a centralized operating model, yet 67% use at least 11 lab vendors. 73% are adding locations and standardizing operations at the same time.

03

The biggest obstacles are operational and clinical.

Disruption during transition (71%) and clinician preference or technician relationships (67%) lead the barriers. Nearly half (48%) question whether a new vendor can match current quality.

04

Technology is a foundation that is not yet fully used.

All respondents use scanners in at least half their locations, but only 28% report full-network coverage. The opportunity is consistent, supported workflows and usable performance data.

The executive implication

Treat consolidation as a multi-function operating change. Establish a baseline, win clinician participation, test a defined scope and release further investment only when the evidence supports expansion.

Scope. 115 senior lab decision-makers at DSOs with 4–50 locations; 80% report majority private-equity backing and 58% hold operations leadership roles. The findings describe the emerging DSO segment surveyed, not the entire market. Source: Dandy/Isurus Emerging DSO Survey, August 2026, slides 5, 15, 25, 28–29, 40. Stage n=110.

Central authority has not removed local variation.

All 115 respondents reported centralized operational and purchasing decisions. But policy is only one part of an operating model. Day-to-day choices by providers and practice teams decide how consistently it is carried out.

Exhibit 02Growth model of surveyed organizations Survey data

Source: Dandy/Isurus survey, slide 15. n=115; single-choice question.

The research identified inconsistent purchasing, provider-specific lab preferences and variation in practice performance as obstacles to organizational goals. Together they explain how centralization, as leadership describes it, can coexist with fragmentation on the ground.

A preferred-vendor agreement can simplify contracting while leaving the clinical workflow unchanged. Unless it changes how cases are prescribed, submitted, tracked and supported, headquarters has limited visibility into the results of its purchasing strategy.

The answer is to separate decision rights from execution. Leadership sets a preferred pathway and negotiates terms. Practice managers and clinicians need a credible workflow, access to support and a way to surface exceptions. Clinical judgment stays with the treating provider.

Measure the difference

Track the share of eligible cases sent through the preferred workflow, not the share of locations covered by a signed agreement.

Disruption and clinician trust outweigh switching cost.

These barriers describe a change-management problem. A price comparison or a preferred-vendor announcement does not answer what happens to a case already in progress, how staff learn a new submission process or whether a doctor can reach a familiar technical resource on a complex case.

Design the transition before it begins. Agree which cases will switch, document support and escalation paths, keep existing arrangements where clinically necessary and phase the change so one location’s learning informs the next.

Decision test

Before approving a broad rollout, ask clinical and operations leaders which risks are mitigated, which remain open and who has authority to pause or adapt implementation.

03 / Why consolidation stalls

A preferred lab agreement only works if doctors prefer it.

The individual clinician links network strategy to the actual case workflow. 67% name clinician preferences or technician relationships as a major barrier, and 48% name confidence in matching current quality.

Exhibit 06Three elements of a clinician-centered transition Dandy guidance
Design elementWhat leadership should requireSignal it is working
Trust in qualityCompare similar case types against agreed clinical criteria. Review remakes, adjustments and turnaround with clinicians.Clinicians can see their own comparable results.
Workflow confidenceRole-based onboarding and clear paths to live technical help. Make the first cases easy to support.Support requests fall after the first weeks.

Design elements are Dandy’s interpretation, not measured survey outcomes. Barrier data: slide 29, n=115.

Lab relationships hold accumulated knowledge about a clinician’s preferences and the realities of a practice. Replacing one changes more than an invoice. It changes how feedback is exchanged, how issues are resolved and how confidently a doctor sends a case.

Include a representative set of providers in any pilot, not only the most enthusiastic digital adopters. Mix scanner experience, case mix and current lab preferences.

Give clinical leadership a structured way to record quality concerns and to separate onboarding issues from persistent performance problems. The aim is a preferred workflow that earns sustained use. Read adoption alongside quality and patient-care measures; more case share at the expense of clinical performance is not a successful transition.

Clinical guardrail

Make quality and clinician confidence non-negotiable stage gates. No volume target should override an individual treatment decision.

05 / The economics

Judge the economics on the full cost of a completed case.

Financial accountability is prominent: 72% of respondents select EBITDA margin among their personal metrics and 32% select lab or dental supply spend as a percentage of revenue. Consolidation should be judged on the invoice and on the resources it takes to deliver care.

Exhibit 09A framework for evaluating the economics of consolidation Dandy guidance
Economic leverWhat to measureTreatment in the business case
Direct lab spendComparable net invoice cost by product and case mixRealized cost change
Remakes and repeat workIncremental lab, material and shipping costsAvoided direct costs; avoid double counting
Chair and staff timeAdditional appointments, adjustments, support and admin timeReport capacity released separately from cash savings
Capital and transitionEquipment, training, integration, contract and implementation costsOne-time and recurring costs
Incremental productionAdditional completed care from freed capacityScenario-based contribution, not guaranteed revenue

Accountability metrics: slide 18, n=115, up to three selections. The framework is Dandy’s recommended approach; the survey does not quantify ROI.

The unit of analysis is a comparable completed case, not the lowest listed price. Evaluate cost by product and practice, with consistent definitions over a clearly defined period. Measure remake rate and turnaround alongside financial metrics, because changes in quality or service can change the economics of a transition.

Capacity released is not automatically revenue realized. Model how much freed chair time can realistically be filled, account for demand and staffing, and use contribution margin rather than gross production when estimating earnings impact.

Board-level discipline

Report realized savings, released capacity and potential incremental contribution as separate lines. Subtract transition costs. Use sensitivity ranges instead of treating an upside case as a forecast.

06 / Closing the gap

Start small. Measure everything. Scale on evidence.

Structure consolidation as three successive investment decisions. Each stage produces an evidence package that decides whether, where and how the organization expands.

Exhibit 12A measured implementation framework Dandy guidance
01

Assess

Establish the baseline and define the eligible scope. Map vendor use, spend, case types, scanner coverage and clinical exceptions.

OutputBaseline, business case and agreed pilot criteria.
02

Pilot

Test the workflow at a small, representative group of practices. Train clinicians and staff; monitor first cases, support needs and outcomes.

OutputEvidence on quality, adoption, cost and readiness.
03

Scale

Expand in phases using the pilot’s learning. Prioritize ready locations, reuse the training model and track each new cohort.

OutputPhased rollout plan, governance and ongoing reporting.

Dandy’s recommended framework. The survey did not test the effectiveness of this framework or any particular pilot duration.

Assess: a defensible starting point

Build a practice- and product-level inventory of lab activity. Reconcile invoice and case data, identify contracts and exceptions, and agree the metrics leadership and clinicians care about. Record baseline performance over a representative period.

Pilot: test the operating model

Dandy recommends starting with 3–10 practices, depending on group size and readiness. Mix providers, define eligible cases and agree evaluation windows and escalation procedures. Compare to baseline, adjusting for product mix.

Scale: expand where evidence supports it

Review results with finance, operations and clinical leadership. Resolve material quality or workflow issues first. Keep exception paths, cohort reporting and a feedback loop so each rollout improves on the last.

Governance turns a pilot into a repeatable program.

Consolidation crosses organizational boundaries. Clear ownership and a regular decision cadence keep procurement decisions, practice-level implementation and clinical judgment aligned.

Exhibit 15A practical governance model Dandy guidance
Accountable roleResponsibilities
CEO / executive sponsorSet objectives, resolve cross-functional trade-offs and approve expansion decisions.
Finance leadershipValidate the baseline and economic case; oversee commercial terms and realized value.
Operations leadershipOwn readiness, training coordination, rollout sequencing and issue management.
Clinical leadershipDefine clinical guardrails, evaluate quality, engage providers and govern exceptions.
Practice championsSupport local onboarding, report workflow friction and capture provider feedback.
Lab partnerProvide implementation support, agreed performance reporting and escalation ownership.

Dandy guidance.

Set a rhythm for decisions. During the pilot, review operational issues often enough to resolve barriers while cases are still in progress. Use a cross-functional checkpoint to review evidence against the scorecard. For expansion, track results by onboarding cohort and review capacity, adoption and quality before bringing more practices live.

Make exceptions visible and constructive. A clinical exception is not necessarily an adoption failure. Record the reason, ask whether the preferred pathway can improve and preserve flexibility when a case warrants it. Separate clinically necessary exceptions from avoidable operational friction.

Executive action

Appoint one accountable program owner with named finance and clinical decision-makers, and give them explicit authority to resolve issues and delay rollout if guardrails are not met.
